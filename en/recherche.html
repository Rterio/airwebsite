From the clinic to the lab, and back

Our research questions come from clinical practice, and our findings go back to it to change how patients are cared for.

  1. Observe in the clinic

    Patients followed at the IUCPQ show us where current diagnosis and treatment fall short.

  2. Measure inflammation

    Biomarkers that are easy to obtain in the clinic (blood, sputum, exhaled air) describe each patient's inflammation.

  3. Group the profiles

    These measurements identify subgroups of patients with similar underlying mechanisms, called endotypes.

  4. Target the treatment

    Each endotype points to the therapy most likely to work, including biologic treatments.

The findings go back to the clinic, where they raise new questions.

Severe asthma and airway inflammation

About one in ten people with asthma has a severe form of the disease that stays hard to control despite high doses of medication. Attacks (exacerbations) are frequent and often treated with oral corticosteroids, whose side effects add up over time.

We study what happens in the airways during these attacks, so each one can be treated according to its cause rather than in the same way every time.

Questions we're asking

  • What dose of oral corticosteroids is actually needed to treat an exacerbation?
  • Can we tell, at the time of an attack, which ones need antibiotics and which don't?
  • How does inflammation change in patients treated with biologic medications?

Faster care pathways

Many people live with respiratory symptoms for years before being diagnosed with asthma, then wait a long time to see a specialist. These delays take a toll on quality of life and lead to more medical visits.

We design and evaluate models of care that shorten the path from first symptoms to diagnosis and the right treatment.

Questions we're asking

  • How many people live with undiagnosed asthma, and what is the impact?
  • Can a clinic dedicated to exacerbations reduce medication use without harming patients?
  • How can objective asthma testing be better built into primary care?

Inflammatory phenotyping

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease: two people with the same symptoms can have very different inflammatory mechanisms, and so respond differently to treatment.

We aim to identify subgroups of patients, or endotypes, using biomarkers that are easy to obtain in the clinic, linking what we see in the patient to what is happening in their airways.

Questions we're asking

  • Which simple biomarkers can predict response to a targeted treatment?
  • How does the inflammatory profile change as asthma progresses?
  • Can these profiles help in developing new medications?

Featured projects

A selection of projects led or co-led by the team

  • Model of care Prix Hippocrate 2025

    AllianC5: Clinic for the Assessment of Asthma Exacerbations

    A clinic unique in Canada that gives people living with severe asthma a fast, objective assessment of their attacks and a treatment tailored to each one.

    −50%oral corticosteroid use
    −41%antibiotic use

    Read the IUCPQ article (in French)

  • Clinical trial Pilot trial

    DOSE study: the right steroid dose for an asthma attack

    Guidelines recommend widely varying doses of oral corticosteroids for an exacerbation, and few studies have compared them. This randomized pilot trial compares three prednisone regimens, lasting 3 to 10 days, in patients with severe asthma.

    ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT06833814)

  • National study Published

    UCAP: finding undiagnosed asthma and COPD

    A large Canadian study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, on early detection of asthma and COPD in primary care. The teams of Drs. Louis-Philippe Boulet and Andréanne Côté recruited more than one hundred of the 508 participants.

    Learn more on the IUCPQ website

  • Guidelines Contribution

    Clinical recommendations on severe asthma and cough

    Dr. Côté is a member of the Canadian Thoracic Society's Asthma Committee. She contributed to the CTS Severe Asthma Position Statement and the CHEST cough guidelines.

Methods and expertise

The assessment tools we use with participants

Spirometry
Measures breathing and airway obstruction, before and after a bronchodilator.
Bronchial challenge tests
Assess how sensitive the airways are, notably to methacholine.
Induced sputum
Analysis of inflammatory cells in the airways.
Exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)
A simple, non-invasive marker of type 2 inflammation.
Blood biomarkers
Eosinophils, IgE and other markers measured with a simple blood test.
Validated questionnaires
Asthma control, quality of life and day-to-day symptoms.
